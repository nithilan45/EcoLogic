\section{Proofs}\label{app:proofs}

\paragraph{Proposition~\ref{prop:ladder} (ladder).}
The inequalities follow from $\Pi_0\subset\Pi_1\subset\Pi_2$: a constant is a
function of $x$, and a function of $x$ is a function of $(x,u,c)$. For concavity,
$A$ and $C$ are linear in $z$ and each class is convex and closed under mixing,
so if $z^1$ is feasible at $b_1$ and $z^2$ at $b_2$ then $tz^1+(1-t)z^2$ is
feasible at $tb_1+(1-t)b_2$ and attains at least $tA(z^1)+(1-t)A(z^2)$.
Monotonicity holds because enlarging $b$ enlarges the feasible set. \hfill$\square$

\paragraph{Proposition~\ref{prop:rho} ($\rho=1$ iff outcomes are deterministic given $x$).}
If $u,c$ are $\sigma(x)$-measurable then $\Pi_1=\Pi_2$ as policy classes with the
same value and cost functionals, so $A^\dagger=A^*$ and $\rho\equiv1$.
\hfill$\square$

\paragraph{Two-model closed forms.}
Assume constant per-model costs $c_s<c_l$ and $\E[u_l]\ge\E[u_s]$, and let
$\beta$ be the traffic fraction sent to $l$, so $b(\beta)=(1-\beta)c_s+\beta c_l$.
(a) A query-independent policy mixes the two points and its value is linear and
increasing in $\beta$, so the budget binds and $\Sb=\E[u_s]+\beta\E[D]$.
(b) Maximising $\E[u_s]+\E[z\delta]$ over $z:\mathcal X\to[0,1]$ with
$\E[z]\le\beta$ is a fractional knapsack whose solution sets $z=1$ on the largest
values of $\delta$, giving $\beta\CTE_\beta(\delta)$.
(c) Identical, with the oracle ranking by realised $D$.
(d) Binary case: any policy has value at most $\E[\max(u_s,u_l)]=1-p_{00}$,
attained by the cheapest-correct oracle; since $\E[u_s]=p_{11}+p_{10}$ and
$\E[u_l]=p_{11}+p_{01}$ we get $(1-p_{00})-\E[u_s]=p_{01}$ and
$(1-p_{00})-\E[u_l]=p_{10}$, so the gain over the better single model is
$\min(p_{01},p_{10})$. \hfill$\square$

Since $\delta=\E[D\mid x]$ and $\CTE_\beta$ is a coherent risk measure,
conditioning contracts it, which re-proves $\rho\le1$ independently of
Proposition~\ref{prop:ladder}.

\medskip
Note that $\min(p_{01},p_{10})$ is \emph{not} $\kappa(b)$: it measures headroom
over the better single model, whereas $\kappa(b)$ measures headroom over the
matched-cost \emph{mixture}, which is worse than the better single model whenever
$\beta<1$. A router can gain over a mixture even when $\min(p_{01},p_{10})=0$, by
spending a fixed budget where it does the most good. We report both because
$\kappa(b)$ is the quantity in the decomposition and $\min(p_{01},p_{10})$ is the
quantity a reader intuits.

\paragraph{Proposition~\ref{prop:ceiling} (ceiling).} In the main text.
The variance step is the fact that a $[0,1]$-valued random variable with mean
$\beta$ has variance at most $\beta(1-\beta)$, attained by the two-point
$\{0,1\}$ distribution \citep{bhatia2000variance}. The maximum of
$\sqrt{\beta(1-\beta)}$ over $\beta$ is at $\beta=1/2$.

\paragraph{Proposition~\ref{prop:reps} (identification).}
The full statement, condensed in the body. Let $\hat\eta_m$ be the mean of the
$k$ replicates of model $m$ on an item. Then
$\Var(\hat\eta_m)=\Var(\eta_m)+\tfrac1k\E[\eta_m(1-\eta_m)]$ and
$\E\bigl[\tfrac{k}{k-1}\hat\eta_m(1-\hat\eta_m)\bigr]=\E[\eta_m(1-\eta_m)]$,
so with $\delta=\eta_l-\eta_s$,
\[
\widehat\Var(\delta)=\Var(\hat\eta_l-\hat\eta_s)
-\tfrac{1}{k-1}\overline{\left[\hat\eta_l(1-\hat\eta_l)+\hat\eta_s(1-\hat\eta_s)\right]}
\]
is unbiased for $\Var(\delta)$, where the bar denotes the average over items.

\emph{Proof.} The first identity is the law of total variance with
$\Var(\hat\eta\mid x)=\eta(1-\eta)/k$. For the second, $k\hat\eta\sim
\mathrm{Bin}(k,\eta)$ given $x$, so
$\E[\hat\eta(1-\hat\eta)\mid x]=\frac{k-1}{k}\eta(1-\eta)$ and
$\frac{k}{k-1}\hat\eta(1-\hat\eta)$ is unbiased for $\eta(1-\eta)$. Combining
across the two models under conditional independence, with
$\frac1k\cdot\frac{k}{k-1}=\frac1{k-1}$, gives the stated estimator.
\hfill$\square$

\paragraph{Proposition~\ref{prop:auc} (Bayes AUC).}
Score by $\eta$, which is AUC-optimal. With $u=\eta(X)$, $v=\eta(X')$ write
$T_>=\E[\mathbf 1\{u>v\}u(1-v)]$, $T_<=\E[\mathbf 1\{u<v\}u(1-v)]$,
$T_==\E[\mathbf 1\{u=v\}u(1-v)]$. Under the half-credit convention the AUC is
$(T_>+T_=/2)/(a(1-a))$, and $T_>+T_<+T_==\E[u(1-v)]=a(1-a)$. By exchangeability
$T_<=\E[\mathbf 1\{u>v\}v(1-u)]$, so
$T_>-T_<=\E[\mathbf 1\{u>v\}(u-v)]=\tfrac12\E|u-v|$. Solving the two equations
gives $T_>+T_=/2=\tfrac12 a(1-a)+\tfrac14\E|u-v|$, hence the closed form; the
bound follows from $\E|u-v|\le\sqrt{\E(u-v)^2}=\sqrt2\,\mathrm{sd}(\eta)$.
Sanity checks: $\eta$ constant gives $1/2$; $\eta\in\{0,1\}$ equiprobable gives
$1$. \hfill$\square$

they govern (\texttt{stage11\_13/prereg\_stage11\_13.md}); the commit precedes

follows the same discipline as two earlier pre-registrations in the project.

Eight deviations are recorded in \texttt{stage11\_13/DEVIATIONS.md}. The three
that affect claims:

\textbf{D1.} H3 (``the router-free bound is $\le50\%$ of $\kappa$'') was
\textbf{refuted}: the bound is $2.82\times$ $\kappa$ at median. The
pre-registration named this outcome in advance and required us to retreat from
the ceiling framing, which \S\ref{sec:ceiling} does. The replacement claim ---
difficulty is reliable but not inferable from text --- is the one the data
supports.

\textbf{D2.} The pre-registration asserted that a policy seeing one replicate
$Y^{(1)}$ upper-bounds $A^\dagger$. \emph{This is wrong}: $Y^{(1)}$ is a realised
outcome, not a function of $x$, so the two information sets are incomparable; only
their join contains $\sigma(x)$. The error was caught while implementing Stage 12,
before any result depended on it. The quantity is still reported, relabelled as
the unbiased value of a feasible peeking policy rather than a bound.

\textbf{D8.} The pre-registered fine-tuned generative LLM rung is
\textsc{blocked}; Appendix~\ref{app:blocked} gives the four provider errors.
Two additions are exploratory and labelled as such throughout: the learning
curves (D3) and the end-to-end fine-tuned encoders (D7). The encoders are judged
against the \emph{same} pre-registered $+0.05$ threshold as the pre-registered
rungs, so adding them cannot make the criterion easier to pass.

\section{Further limitations and assumptions}\label{app:limits}

The limitations a reviewer most needs are in the body; these are the remaining
assumptions, stated so they can be checked rather than taken on trust.

\emph{Replicate exchangeability.} Proposition~\ref{prop:reps} assumes replicates
are conditionally i.i.d.\ given the item and independent across models. Positive
within-item correlation --- for instance a prompt that reliably pushes a model
into the same wrong reasoning path --- would understate the noise term and hence
\emph{overstate} $\mathrm{sd}(\delta)$ and the ceiling. The error therefore runs
in routing's favour, so it cannot manufacture our negative direction; it could
however make the vacuousness of Proposition~\ref{prop:ceiling} look worse than
it is.

\emph{Which propositions are general.} Propositions~\ref{prop:ladder},
\ref{prop:rho}, \ref{prop:reps} and \ref{prop:auc} hold for arbitrary $K$.
Proposition~\ref{prop:ceiling} and the closed-form frontier expressions are
two-model statements; with $K>2$ we compute the frontiers by linear program
instead (\S\ref{app:validate} checks the two agree at $K=2$).

\emph{The learning curve is a fit, not a bound.} $\mathrm{AUC}(n)=A-Bn^{-c}$ is
fitted by least squares on nine training sizes over roughly two decades of $n$.
Its asymptote $A=0.741$ is an extrapolation whose functional form we chose; a
different form with the same in-range fit can put the asymptote elsewhere. We
use it to argue that the observed trend is slow, not to prove a ceiling.

\emph{Three models is not a model ecosystem.} The reliability numbers come from
one small reasoning model, one mid-tier model and GPT-4o. They should be
re-measured before being assumed for a different tier structure, and nothing in
this paper establishes that reliability is stable across model families.

\section{Matched-cost baselines and the biased cost axis}\label{sec:cost}

The two measurement corrections summarised at the end of \S\ref{sec:eps}, in
full. Both are prior stages of this project rather than contributions of this
paper, but every number in \S\ref{sec:rb}--\ref{sec:eps} depends on them.

\paragraph{The baseline.} On our own 364-item frozen test set, a deployed
keyword router is beaten on \emph{both} axes simultaneously by the constant
policy ``send everything to the mid tier'': $86.8\%$ vs $92.3\%$ accuracy at
$6.87\times$ the measured dollar cost. Its headline saving against
always-frontier is $88.6\%$ in modelled joules but $55.0\%$ in measured
dollars---the choice of cost axis moves the number by $4\times$ on identical
routing decisions. Applying the same audit to RouteLLM
\citep{ong2024routellm} on its own released GSM8K data, its router \emph{does}
beat a cost-matched query-independent mixture, by a mean of $+0.57\pp$: positive
at 8 of 9 operating points (sign test $p=0.039$) but significant at none
individually ($n=1{,}307$). So the protocol discriminates rather than rejects---
and even the passing case has an effect the field's benchmarks cannot resolve
point-by-point, while being reported as ``up to $85\%$ cost reduction''.

\paragraph{The axis.} Pricing a routing policy by multiplying traffic fractions
by per-model \emph{mean} cost is exact for a query-independent policy but biased
for a router, because routers escalate precisely the items that emit more
tokens. The exact correction is
\[
R_{\text{true}}=R_{\text{naive}}+\sum_{i\ne j}\Cov\bigl(\mathbf 1\{t^*{=}i,\hat t{=}j\},\,e_j(x)-e_i(x)\bigr).
\]
On our data this term is $1.9\times$ the regret being reported and flips its
sign, reconciling to $2\times10^{-16}$; on RouteLLM's own GSM8K data the sign
flip reproduces and naive accounting overstates savings by up to $6.3\%$. The
bias always favours the router and grows with within-model cost dispersion, so
it worsens as the field routes among reasoning models with variable-length
thinking budgets. All routers in this paper are charged realised per-item cost.

Note that this correction is the \emph{same covariance object} as
Proposition~\ref{prop:ceiling}: routing lives or dies on one covariance between
a routing indicator and a per-item difference, read once on the utility axis and
once on the cost axis.
